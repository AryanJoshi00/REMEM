Newton's Laws of Motion (Physics)

Introduction
Newton's Laws of Motion are three fundamental laws that explain the relationship between the motion of an object and the forces acting on it.

These laws were introduced by Sir Isaac Newton and form the foundation of classical mechanics.

First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)
An object remains at rest or continues moving in a straight line at constant speed unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

Example:
- A book placed on a table remains at rest until someone moves it.
- Passengers move forward when a moving bus suddenly stops.
Inertia: The tendency of an object to resist any change in its state of rest or motion.

Second Law of Motion (Law of Acceleration)
The acceleration of an object depends on the net force applied and its mass.

The acceleration increases when the applied force increases and decreases when the mass increases.

Formula: F = ma
Where:
- F = Force (Newton)
- m = Mass (kg)
- a = Acceleration (m/s²)
Example:
- An empty shopping cart is easier to push than a fully loaded cart.
- A football moves faster when kicked with greater force.

Third Law of Motion (Action and Reaction)
For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

This means that whenever one object applies a force on another object, the second object applies an equal force in the opposite direction.

Examples:
- When we walk, our feet push the ground backwards, and the ground pushes us forward.
- A rocket moves upward by pushing gases downward.
- A swimmer pushes water backwards to move forward.

Applications of Newton's Laws
1. Transportation: Helps in understanding the motion and braking of vehicles.
2. Sports: Explains how force affects the movement of balls and athletes.
3. Rocket Science: Helps explain rocket propulsion.
4. Engineering: Used in designing machines, bridges and vehicles.
5. Everyday Activities: Explains walking, running, jumping and pushing objects.
