Cognitive Biases: how our thinking takes shortcuts (Psychology)

Introduction
A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern in the way people think that leads to errors in judgement. Biases often come from heuristics, which are mental shortcuts that help the brain make quick decisions with limited information. The shortcuts usually work, but sometimes they mislead us.

Anchoring bias
- Anchoring bias is relying too heavily on the first piece of information we receive.
- Example: a shirt with an original price of 2000 rupees marked down to 1000 seems cheap, even if it is worth only 500.
- Negotiators use anchoring by naming the first number.

Confirmation bias
- Confirmation bias is the tendency to look for, notice and remember information that supports what we already believe.
- Example: someone who thinks a team is unlucky remembers every loss and forgets the wins.
- It makes it hard to change our minds, because evidence against our view is ignored or dismissed.

Sunk cost fallacy
- The sunk cost fallacy is continuing something because of the time, money or effort already spent, even when stopping would be better.
- Example: watching a boring film to the end because you already paid for the ticket.
- The past cost cannot be recovered, so the choice should depend only on future benefits.

Availability heuristic
- The availability heuristic is judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind.
- Example: after news reports about a plane crash, people think flying is more dangerous than driving, even though driving causes far more deaths.
- Vivid, recent or emotional events feel more common than they are.

Dunning-Kruger effect
- The Dunning-Kruger effect is the tendency of people with low skill in an area to overestimate their own ability.
- Beginners often do not know enough to see their own mistakes.
- Experts, by contrast, can underestimate how much they know compared with others.

Framing effect
- The framing effect is when our choice changes depending on how the same information is presented.
- Example: people prefer a treatment described as saving 90 percent of patients over the same treatment described as losing 10 percent of patients.
- The facts are identical, but a gain frame and a loss frame feel different.
